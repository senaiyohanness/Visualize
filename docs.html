Spotify Track Visualization Project

Design Process

I began with a dataset of 2,500 Spotify songs. There was extra attributes which I removed. My initial idea was to make an interactive tool that helps people explore how songs vary across genres and over time. At first, I sketched out a basic scatter plot, but I realized they would be too limiting. Instead, I landed on a scatterplot, which is betterer for comparing any two quantitative features like danceability, energy, and duration_ms.

I placed the filters and controls on the right so they wouldn’t crowd the chart and would be easily accessible without hiding data. As I coded in D3.js, I tested different axis combinations and ensured the tooltip gave enough detail without cluttering the interface. Once the interaction felt smooth, I moved on to styling, tooltips, and final adjustments before deploying to GitHub Pages.

Initial Sketch

I started with a sketch on paint for windows to basically figure out where filters, axis controls, and the legend would go, And so below is my original sketch idea. I initially planned to represent popularity using circle size so, larger = more popular, but I found that this approach made it hard to see smaller songs, especially when popular ones overlapped them. I removed that idea from the final version just because it makes more sense honestly.

Design Choices

  • Scatterplot: great for comparing two numeric features and showing patterns naturally.
  • Color encoding: used to represent genre, making it easy to visually group songs by type.
  • Dropdowns: allow users to change X and Y axes dynamically so, danceability, energy, duration_ms.
  • Filters: users can filter by genre or year to explore more focused subsets of data.
  • Tooltips: show title, artist, genre, explicit status, and duration — giving context on hover.
  • Layout: filters on the right side to keep the chart clean and interaction intuitive.

Findings and Discoveries

By exploring the scatterplot, I noticed that genres like EDM and hip hop consistently score high in both energy and danceability, which supports the idea that they’re made for movement and rhythm. In contrast, folk and world music genres generally occupy the lower end of the energy spectrum, with a more relaxed style.

Another pattern emerged when filtering by year recent years after 2015 have slightly higher danceability averages, which probably means like the trend of music changed and was more popular, which is true because that era was full of dancing songs!

When switching the axes to duration_ms, it became clear that duration doesn’t vary much by genre but shows interesting outliers especially very short or long tracks in experimental genres.
